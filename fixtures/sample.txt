CHAPTER I.
The Lighthouse Keeper

The lighthouse at Marrow Point had stood for ninety years, and in all
that time it had never once failed to burn. Old Tobias Wren, who tended
it, said that a light was a promise, and a promise was not a thing to be
broken because one happened to be tired.

Every evening he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps with a can of
oil in each hand, counting them under his breath as his father had
taught him. At the top the lamp waited, patient and enormous, a great
cut-glass eye that looked out over the grey water.

On the night this story begins the wind came from the north-east, and it
brought with it a thin cold rain that rattled on the gallery like
handfuls of thrown gravel. Tobias trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork,
and settled to his watch.

Far below, the sea heaved itself against the rocks and fell back again,
over and over, with the slow patience of something that has all the time
in the world.


CHAPTER II.
A Stranger on the Shore

It was past midnight when Tobias saw the figure on the shingle. At first
he took it for a trick of the rain, a bundle of weed or a fallen spar,
but the shape moved, and lifted one arm, and he understood that someone
was alive down there.

He went down faster than he had gone in twenty years, a lantern swinging
in his fist and the cold biting at his lungs. The stranger was a young
woman wrapped in a sodden coat, and she was shivering so hard that she
could not speak.

He carried her to the cottage at the foot of the tower and set her by
the stove. When at last she could talk, she told him that her name was
Imogen Hale, that her small boat had been driven onto the rocks, and
that she had been rowing toward the light for three hours.

"I never took my eyes from it," she said. "Whenever I lost hope I looked
up, and there it was."

Tobias said nothing, but he stood a little straighter, and went back up
the stairs to his lamp with something warm lodged in his chest.


CHAPTER III.
What the Tide Brought

By morning the storm had blown itself out, and the sea lay flat and
silver under a washed sky. Imogen walked the beach with a borrowed pair
of boots, gathering what the tide had delivered: planks, rope, a single
oar, and a brass-bound chest that was far too heavy to lift.

Together they dragged it above the high-water line. The lock had rusted
through, and a few blows with a hammer broke it open. Inside, wrapped in
oilcloth, lay a bundle of letters tied with blue ribbon and a small,
battered compass.

The letters were addressed to a ship's captain named Edmund Hale. Imogen
read the first one in silence, and then she sat down on the chest and
wept, for Edmund Hale had been her grandfather, lost at sea long before
she was born.

The compass still worked. Its needle swung, steadied, and pointed
unwaveringly at the lighthouse.


CHAPTER IV.
Letters in Blue Ribbon

They read the letters over the course of a week, one each evening, by
the light of the kitchen lamp. They were written to a wife who waited
ashore, and they were full of small things: a gull that had followed the
ship for a hundred miles, a cook who sang badly, the strange green
colour of the water near the equator.

In the last letter the captain wrote of a storm that was gathering, and
of a light he hoped to see before dawn. "If I do not come home," he
wrote, "tell the child that I looked for the lighthouse until the very
end."

Tobias set down the page and looked at the window, where the great beam
was beginning to sweep across the dusk. He had never known who the light
was for. He had thought, in his plainer moments, that it was for no one
in particular.

Now he understood that it had always been for someone: for every sailor
who looked up in the dark and found it there.


CHAPTER V.
The Long Way Home

When the supply boat came on Friday, Imogen went back with it to the
mainland, carrying the letters and the compass in her coat. She left
behind her address, written in a careful hand, and a promise to return
in the spring.

The winter was long, and the nights were longer, and Tobias kept his
light burning through gales and fogs and the black stillness of frost.
He found that he no longer climbed the stairs alone; he climbed them
with the thought of all the unseen faces turned toward the glow.

In April, as the first crocuses opened in the lee of the cottage wall, a
small boat appeared on the horizon. It was a sturdy craft with a red
sail, and in the bow stood Imogen, waving both arms above her head.

He waved back. It seemed to him then that the light had finished one
long errand and begun another.


CHAPTER VI.
Marrow Point at Dusk

Imogen stayed that summer, and the summer after. She learned to trim the
wick and wind the clockwork, and to read the weather in the colour of
the evening sky. She had a gift for it, Tobias said, the same gift her
grandfather must have had.

In time the Trinity House inspectors came, in their polished boots and
serious hats, and were surprised to find two keepers where there had
been one. They inspected the brass and the glass and the logbook, and
pronounced everything in excellent order.

On the evening they left, Tobias and Imogen climbed the tower together
and watched the lamp kindle. The beam swept out across the water, steady
and unhurried, and far off a ship's lantern answered it.

"A promise," said Tobias quietly, "is not a thing to be broken."

And the light burned on.
